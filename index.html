22.1

Electric fields

Learning outcomes
  • Describe an electric field as a region where a charge experiences a force, and draw field lines for simple arrangements.
  • Define electric field strength and use E = F/Q.
  • Use E = V/d for the uniform field between parallel plates.
  • Describe and calculate the motion of a charged particle that enters a uniform field parallel or perpendicular to it.

What an electric field is

A charge changes the space around it. Any other charge placed there feels a force, even with nothing touching it. We call the region where this happens an electric field. Electric fields are produced by charges, and a charge is the only thing that responds to one.

Electric field strength

The electric field strength at a point is the force per unit positive charge acting on a small stationary test charge placed at that point.

E = FQunit: N C−1 (equivalent to V m−1). A vector, pointing the way a positive charge would be pushed.

Rearranged, the force on any charge Q in a field is F = QE. A positive charge is pushed along the field; a negative charge is pushed against it.

Field lines

  • The tangent to a line gives the direction of the force on a positive charge.
  • Lines that are close together mean a strong field; lines that are far apart mean a weak one.
  • Lines begin on positive charge and end on negative charge (or run to infinity). They never cross.
  • Lines meet the surface of a conductor at right angles.

The uniform field between parallel plates

Two flat metal plates, one at each potential, produce a field with parallel, equally spaced lines in the middle. The strength is the same everywhere there. Near the edges the lines bulge outwards ("fringing") and the field is no longer uniform.

E = VdV = potential difference between the plates, d = their separation (in metres).

Charged particles in a uniform field

The force QE is constant, so the acceleration a = QE/m is constant too. Two cases matter.

  • Parallel to the field. Straight-line motion with uniform acceleration. Use the equations of motion.
  • Perpendicular to the field. Treat the two directions separately, as for a projectile. The speed across the plates stays constant, t = L/v, and the particle accelerates sideways. The path is a parabola.
y = ½at2 = QEL22mv2sideways displacement after crossing plates of length L
Worked example

An electron enters the space between two parallel plates at 2.0 × 107 m s−1, parallel to the plates. The plates are 5.0 cm long and 3.0 cm apart, with a p.d. of 200 V. Find the sideways deflection on leaving.

  1. E = V/d = 200 / 0.030 = 6.7 × 103 V m−1
  2. a = eE/m = (1.60 × 10−19 × 6.67 × 103) / 9.11 × 10−31 = 1.2 × 1015 m s−2
  3. t = L/v = 0.050 / 2.0 × 107 = 2.5 × 10−9 s
  4. y = ½at2 = ½ × 1.17 × 1015 × (2.5 × 10−9)2 = 3.7 × 10−3 m = 3.7 mm, towards the positive plate.

Check this in the second simulation. The default settings are these numbers.

Common mistakes
  • Using E = V/d with d in centimetres. Convert to metres first.
  • Sending an electron the wrong way. The force on a negative charge is opposite to E.
  • Treating field strength as a force. It is force per unit charge.

22.2

Coulomb's law

Learning outcomes
  • State Coulomb's law for the force between two point charges in a vacuum.
  • Calculate the magnitude and direction of the force, and how it changes when charge or separation changes.
  • Recognise that the forces on the two charges are equal and opposite.
  • Explain why a charged sphere can be treated as a point charge at its centre.

The law

The electrostatic force between two point charges is proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of their separation.

F = Q1Q24πε0r2in a vacuum (air behaves almost the same). 1/(4πε0) = 8.99 × 109 N m2 C−2

What the law tells you

  • Direction. Like charges repel, unlike charges attract. If you keep the signs in the formula, a positive answer means repulsion and a negative answer means attraction.
  • Inverse square. Double the separation and the force falls to a quarter. Triple it and the force falls to a ninth.
  • Product of charges. Double one charge and the force doubles. Double both and it quadruples.
  • Newton's third law. Each charge pulls or pushes the other with the same size of force, even when the charges are very different.

Point charges and spheres

The law is written for point charges. A uniformly charged sphere acts, for points outside it, as if all its charge were at its centre. So r is the distance between centres, not between surfaces.

More than two charges

Forces from different charges add as vectors. Find the force from each charge on its own, then combine them using components or a scale diagram.

Worked example

Two small spheres carry +3.0 μC and −5.0 μC. They are 20 cm apart in air. Find the force between them, and the force when they are 40 cm apart.

  1. Convert: Q1 = 3.0 × 10−6 C, Q2 = 5.0 × 10−6 C, r = 0.20 m.
  2. F = (8.99 × 109 × 3.0 × 10−6 × 5.0 × 10−6) / 0.202 = 3.4 N, attractive.
  3. At 0.40 m the separation is doubled, so F = 3.37 / 4 = 0.84 N.
Common mistakes
  • Forgetting to convert μC, nC and cm to C and m.
  • Squaring only part of the denominator. The whole separation is squared.
  • Thinking the larger charge feels the larger force. Both feel the same force.

22.3

Electric field strength for a radial field

Learning outcomes
  • Derive and use E = Q/(4πε0r2) for a point charge or charged sphere.
  • Sketch and interpret graphs of E against r and against 1/r2.
  • Combine the fields of two or more charges by vector addition.
  • Compare radial and uniform fields.

From Coulomb's law to field strength

Place a small test charge q at distance r from a point charge Q. Coulomb's law gives the force, and dividing by q gives the field strength.

E = Fq = Q4πε0r2 direction: away from +Q, towards −Q

The field strength depends on the source charge Q and the distance r, not on the test charge. That is why it is a property of the point in space.

Radial fields in graphs

  • E against r is a curve that falls steeply and never reaches zero. Doubling r divides E by 4.
  • E against 1/r2 is a straight line through the origin. Its gradient is Q/(4πε0), so you can find Q from experimental data.

Charged spheres

Outside a charged sphere, the field is the same as for a point charge of the same size at the centre, with r measured from the centre. At the surface, r equals the radius R and the field is at its largest. Inside a conducting sphere the field is zero.

Several charges

Fields add as vectors. Between two equal like charges there is a point where the two fields cancel exactly. Between unlike charges the fields point the same way, so they add.

Uniform fieldRadial field
SourceParallel charged platesPoint charge or sphere
Field linesParallel, equally spacedSpread out from or into the charge
StrengthConstant, E = V/dFalls as 1/r2
Worked example

Find the field strength 0.30 m from a point charge of +2.0 nC.

  1. E = (8.99 × 109 × 2.0 × 10−9) / 0.302
  2. E = 18.0 / 0.090 = 2.0 × 102 N C−1, directed away from the charge.
Common mistakes
  • Using E = V/d for a point charge. That relation is for uniform fields.
  • Measuring r from the surface of a sphere instead of its centre.
  • Adding field strengths as numbers when they point in different directions.

Field of a charged sphere

  2. Switch to the 1/r2 graph. Change Q and watch the gradient.
  3. Make Q negative. What happens to the direction of E and the force on a positive test charge?

22.4

Electric potential

Learning outcomes
  • Define electric potential and use V = Q/(4πε0r) for a point charge.
  • Calculate work done and potential energy changes using W = qΔV.
  • Relate field strength to the gradient of a potential graph, and potential difference to the area under a field graph.
  • Describe equipotential surfaces and how they relate to field lines.

Potential

The electric potential at a point is the work done per unit positive charge in bringing a small test charge from infinity to that point. It is a scalar, measured in volts (J C−1). Potential is zero at infinity.

V = Q4πε0rFor +Q the potential is positive, for −Q it is negative. It falls as 1/r, more slowly than the field does.

Energy and work

A charge q at a point where the potential is V has electric potential energy qV. Moving it between two points changes this energy by qΔV, whatever route is taken.

W = qΔV Ep = qV = Qq4πε0r

If W is positive, an external agent has to do work to move the charge, and the charge stores that energy. If it is negative, the field does the work and the charge gains kinetic energy.

Field and potential

E = −ΔVΔx The field strength is minus the gradient of the V–x graph. The area under an E–x graph gives the potential difference.

For a point charge, V = Q/(4πε0r) has gradient −Q/(4πε0r2), and minus that gradient is exactly the field strength from 22.3. In a uniform field the V–x graph is a straight line, which gives E = V/d.

Equipotentials

An equipotential surface joins points at the same potential. No work is done moving a charge along it, so field lines cross it at right angles. Where equipotentials are close together the potential changes quickly and the field is strong.

Worked example

A proton is moved from 2.0 cm to 1.0 cm from a fixed point charge of +5.0 nC. Find the work done.

  1. V1 = (8.99 × 109 × 5.0 × 10−9) / 0.020 = 2.25 × 103 V
  2. V2 = (8.99 × 109 × 5.0 × 10−9) / 0.010 = 4.50 × 103 V
  3. W = eΔV = 1.60 × 10−19 × 2.25 × 103 = 3.6 × 10−16 J (positive: energy is stored)
Common mistakes
  • Treating potential as a vector. It has a sign but no direction.
  • Dropping the sign of a negative source charge when calculating V.
  • Reading field strength from the height of a V–x graph instead of its gradient.

22.5

Gravitational and electric fields

Learning outcomes
  • Compare gravitational and electric fields: quantities, equations, similarities and differences.
  • Explain why the electric force between particles is vastly stronger than the gravitational force, yet gravity dominates for planets.
  • Explain the sign of gravitational and electric potential.

Same shape, different source

Both fields act at a distance, both follow an inverse-square law for point sources, and both can be described by a field strength and a potential. Many results from 22.3 and 22.4 carry over once you swap mass for charge.

Strength of the constantG = 6.67 × 10−111/(4πε0) = 8.99 × 109

Why gravity is neglected inside atoms

For a proton and an electron in a hydrogen atom, the electric force is about 1039 times the gravitational force. Both forces fall as 1/r2, so this ratio does not depend on separation. It is set by the particles themselves.

Gravity wins at large scales because bulk matter is almost exactly neutral. Positive and negative charges cancel, while masses only add.

The sign of potential

Both potentials are zero at infinity. Gravity is always attractive, so bringing a mass in from infinity is done by the field, and the potential falls below zero. Bringing a positive test charge towards a positive source takes work against repulsion, so the potential rises above zero. Bring it towards a negative source and the potential is negative.

Worked example

In a hydrogen atom the electron is 5.3 × 10−11 m from the proton. Compare the electric and gravitational forces.

  1. FE = (8.99 × 109 × (1.60 × 10−19)2) / (5.3 × 10−11)2 = 8.2 × 10−8 N
  2. FG = (6.67 × 10−11 × 9.11 × 10−31 × 1.67 × 10−27) / (5.3 × 10−11)2 = 3.6 × 10−47 N
  3. FE / FG = 2.3 × 1039
Common mistakes
  • Saying gravity is "weaker" without saying for which particles. For planets it is the main force.
  • Claiming electric force can be shielded but not saying why: free charges in a conductor rearrange to cancel the field.
  • Mixing the units: N kg−1 for g and N C−1 for E.

Electric force against gravitational force

Pick a pair of particles and a separation. Both lines on the log graph fall with the same slope, so the gap between them never changes.

Try this
  1. Pick the proton–electron pair and slide r across the whole range. Does the ratio change?
  2. Switch to two protons. Compare the directions of the two forces.
  3. Pick the 1 kg and 1 C example. Which constant causes the huge ratio, G or 1/(4πε0)?
